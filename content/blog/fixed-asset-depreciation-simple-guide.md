You spend LKR 1,200,000 on a delivery van. Two questions your books have to answer: is that a cost of _this_ year, and is the van still worth 1,200,000 next year? The answer to both is no — and **depreciation** is the mechanism that gets it right. Here's how it works, without the accounting-textbook framing.

## Why a big purchase isn't just an expense

An ordinary expense — rent, electricity, stock — is consumed in the period you pay for it, so it hits that period's profit in full. A **fixed asset** is different: it's something you buy to _use over several years_ — vehicles, machinery, computers, furniture, fit-out. It keeps earning its keep long after you paid for it.

If you dumped the whole LKR 1,200,000 into this year's P&L, this year would look terrible and every following year — when the van is still working for free, on the books — would look artificially good. That's a distorted picture. Depreciation fixes it by **spreading the cost across the years the asset is actually used.**

## The mechanics

When you buy the asset, it goes on the **balance sheet**, not the P&L — you've swapped cash for an asset of equal value, so profit is untouched.

Then, each year (or month), you record a **depreciation charge**: a slice of the cost moves from the balance sheet into the P&L as an expense, and the asset's carrying value on the balance sheet drops by the same slice. The running total of those slices is called **accumulated depreciation**, and:

> Cost − accumulated depreciation = **carrying value** (also called net book value) — what the asset is worth on your books today.

Over the asset's useful life, the carrying value winds down from cost to roughly zero (or to a small residual/scrap value), and the total expense recognised equals what you paid. Nothing is invented; the timing is just spread out.

## The two common methods

**Straight-line.** The same amount every year. Take the cost, subtract any expected residual value, divide by the useful life in years.

_Van: (1,200,000 − 0) ÷ 5 years = LKR 240,000 per year._ Simple, predictable, and the right default for assets that wear evenly — furniture, fit-out, most equipment.

**Reducing balance.** A fixed _percentage_ of the _remaining_ carrying value each year, so the charge is bigger early and smaller later.

_Van at 30% reducing balance: year 1 = 360,000; year 2 = 30% of (1,200,000 − 360,000) = 252,000; and so on._ Better for assets that lose value fastest when new — vehicles, computers, phones.

Neither is "more correct" — pick the one that reflects how the asset actually loses value, apply it consistently, and be able to explain the useful lives you chose. Sri Lankan income tax has its own **capital allowance** rates that may differ from the depreciation in your accounts; that's normal, and the difference is reconciled in the tax computation.

## The fixed asset register

Every fixed asset should sit in a **fixed asset register**: description, purchase date, cost, method, useful life, accumulated depreciation to date, current carrying value, and — eventually — disposal details. When you sell or scrap an asset, you compare what you got for it against its carrying value and book a gain or loss on disposal.

An [auditor](/blog/sme-audit-requirements-sri-lanka) will ask for this register and check that the depreciation in your P&L, the accumulated depreciation on your balance sheet, and the register all agree. If depreciation is a journal entry someone remembers to make each year with no register behind it, that reconciliation becomes a hunt.
