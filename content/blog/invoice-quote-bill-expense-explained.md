"Send me an invoice for that." "Did you enter the bill?" "That's just an expense." New business owners use these words as if they're interchangeable. They're not — each is a distinct document that does a specific thing to your ledger, and mixing them up is how books end up wrong. Here's each one, in order of when it shows up.

## Money coming in (sales side)

### Quote

A **quote** (or estimate) is a _proposal_. "Here's what this would cost." It's not a demand for payment, it commits nobody, and — importantly — **it posts nothing to your accounts.** No revenue, no receivable, nothing. A quote is a sales document that exists so it can later be turned into an invoice if the customer says yes. You can have a hundred outstanding quotes and your books are unchanged.

### Invoice

An **invoice** is a _demand for payment_ for goods or services you've delivered. This one is real accounting. Issuing an invoice:

- records **revenue** (income earned), and
- records a **receivable** (the customer now owes you money).

An invoice is a finalised financial document. Once issued it's [locked](/blog/what-immutable-means-in-accounting-software) — you don't edit it. If it's wrong, you issue a credit note (below). In Sri Lanka, if you're VAT-registered, the invoice also has to follow the [gazette format](/blog/gazette-2481-22-explained) — the word "TAX INVOICE", both parties' tax numbers, a separate supply date.

### Credit note

A **credit note** _reduces_ what a customer owes you — a return, an overcharge, a cancelled line. It's a separate dated document that references the original invoice and posts the opposite entries (reversing revenue and receivable, in whole or part). It's the _only_ correct way to change the effect of an issued invoice.

## Money going out (purchases side)

### Bill

A **bill** is an invoice you _received_ from a supplier — a demand for payment sitting on you. Recording a bill:

- records an **expense** or an **asset** (whatever you bought), and
- records a **payable** (you now owe the supplier).

The key point: a bill is recorded **when you receive it**, not when you pay it. That's what keeps your books showing what you actually owe. Recording bills only on payment day is a [classic SME mistake](/blog/bookkeeping-mistakes-sri-lankan-smes) — it hides your liabilities and flatters your margin.

### Expense (direct expense)

Here "expense" means a purchase you paid for **on the spot**, with no supplier bill to track — a taxi, some stationery, a parking fee paid in cash or on a card. There's no payable because there's nothing outstanding: it's a single entry that records the cost and the money leaving your bank or cash at the same moment. Use a **bill** when you'll pay later; use a **direct expense** when it's already paid.

### Debit note

A **debit note** is the purchases-side mirror of a credit note: it _reduces_ what you owe a supplier — you returned goods, or they overcharged you. Separate dated document, references the original bill, posts the reversing entries.

## Supplier payment (not a document type — an event)

A **supplier payment** isn't a purchase document at all. It's the act of _settling_ one or more bills you already recorded. It moves money out of your bank and reduces the payable — it doesn't create an expense, because the expense was already booked when you entered the bill. One payment can clear several bills at once.

The same is true on the sales side: a **customer payment** settles invoices and reduces the receivable; it doesn't create revenue, because the invoice already did.

## Quick reference

| Document                  | Side      | Creates a payable/receivable? | Posts to P&L?    | When recorded                  |
| ------------------------- | --------- | ----------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Quote                     | Sales     | No                            | No               | Anytime (non-posting)          |
| Invoice                   | Sales     | Receivable                    | Revenue          | When goods/services delivered  |
| Credit note               | Sales     | Reduces receivable            | Reverses revenue | When return/adjustment happens |
| Bill                      | Purchases | Payable                       | Expense/asset    | When bill received             |
| Direct expense            | Purchases | No                            | Expense          | When paid                      |
| Debit note                | Purchases | Reduces payable               | Reverses expense | When return/adjustment happens |
| Customer/supplier payment | Either    | Settles it                    | No               | When money moves               |

## Why the distinctions matter

Get these right and your receivables, payables, revenue and expenses all mean what they say — which is the whole basis of reading your own [P&L and balance sheet](/blog/double-entry-bookkeeping-for-non-accountants). Ledgerly keeps each of these as its own document type with the correct posting built in: a quote posts nothing until you convert it, a bill records the payable the day it arrives, and a payment just settles what's already there. You pick the right document; the ledger entries follow.
