From **Sunday 1 November 2026**, a valid TIN certificate becomes part of everyday business life in Sri Lanka. The 31st falls on a Saturday and the 1st on a Sunday, so next week, **Monday 26 to Friday 30 October**, is the last full working week to get your paperwork in order.

Here's what the IRD has said, what to do next week, and why it's worth putting your TIN to work in your books too.

## What changes on 1 November

According to IRD notice SEC/PN/IT/2026/05 (22 September 2026), the Inland Revenue (Amendment) Act, No. 11 of 2026 means that, from 1 November 2026, people who must hold a TIN have to submit a valid TIN certificate for these transactions:

- opening any account at a bank or financial institution;
- registering a business;
- transferring shares of a company incorporated in Sri Lanka (both the transferor and the transferee);
- obtaining a credit card;
- registering a motor vehicle, or renewing its licence;
- registering land or title to land;
- getting a building plan approved.

**Who it applies to.** The IRD notice says a TIN is mandatory for all resident individuals who were 18 or older on 31 December 2023, or who turn 18 on or after 1 January 2024. Officials handling these transactions are required to check that you've submitted a valid certificate before they process or complete them.

**What the officials are told.** The Daily FT reports that IRD Circular SEC/2026/E/07, which is addressed to banks and other institutions, defines an "account" broadly: savings, current, term deposit and other deposit accounts. For a joint account, each joint holder is considered separately.

**It was meant to start earlier.** KPMG notes that the Act originally had officials asking for the certificate from 1 April 2026. The notice and circular moved the start to 1 November.

**No change announced.** As of 24 October 2026, the IRD website hadn't announced any postponement or change to the 1 November date.

## Don't have the certificate to hand? There's an accepted alternative

The IRD lets you verify your TIN online through its e-Services portal. If you're already registered, you don't need to visit an IRD office.

According to the IRD's TIN verification notice:

- you need your NIC number and either your email address or mobile number;
- you'll receive a one-time password (OTP) to enter;
- once verification is complete, your TIN is shown on screen.

The IRD notice says **a printout of the verification result showing your NIC number and TIN may be accepted instead of the official TIN certificate** for these transactions.

If you don't have a TIN yet, the IRD advises applying through its e-Services TIN registration page.

## Your checklist for 26–30 October

> **Before Friday 30 October**
>
> 1. **Check you have a TIN.** Verify it on IRD e-Services with your NIC and an OTP.
> 2. **Get your proof ready.** Find your TIN certificate, or print the e-Services verification page showing your NIC and TIN.
> 3. **Ask your bank what it needs.** Some banks are already collecting certificates. According to a MyPromo listing, People's Bank asked customers to submit a copy by 31 October.
> 4. **Plan around 1 November.** If you're opening a business account, registering a business, applying for a card or transferring shares soon, have the certificate with you.
> 5. **Partners and joint holders:** each person may need their own TIN certificate.
> 6. **Not sure how it applies to you?** Check with your accountant, or contact the IRD.

## Why this matters beyond the bank counter

A TIN on your bank account links your formal banking to your tax identity. There's a clear direction of travel. In its _Sri Lanka State of the Economy 2026_ report, as reported by the Daily FT, the Institute of Policy Studies says "strengthening taxpayer identification systems, integrating banking and tax information, and using data analytics to identify high-risk taxpayers could significantly improve compliance". The Treasury has also said Budget 2027 is about reforms, not tax cuts.

These are proposals and policy signals, not new IRD rules. The practical point is simple: **what your bank statements show, what your invoices say and what you declare should tell the same story.** That's a bookkeeping job.

## Put your TIN to work in your books

**1. Your own TIN on every tax invoice.** If you're VAT-registered, your TIN, registered name and address have to appear on every Gazette 2481/22 tax invoice. So does your buyer's TIN, when the buyer is VAT-registered. Our [2481/22 checklist](https://www.ledgerly.lk/blog/is-your-vat-invoice-2481-22-compliant) covers the full list.

**2. TINs on customer and supplier records.** Keep the TIN of each VAT-registered customer on file so your tax invoices to them are complete, and so they can claim their input VAT.

**3. TINs for the people you pay.** If you deduct withholding tax from freelancers or contractors, having their details on file helps with the certificates you issue. See our [WHT guide for freelancers](https://www.ledgerly.lk/blog/withholding-tax-freelancers-sri-lanka).

**4. Reconcile your bank account every month.** With a TIN-linked bank account, a business bank balance in your books that reconciles to zero against the statement is the clearest way to show your records and your bank agree.
