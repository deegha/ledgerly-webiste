It's a common plan, especially for owner-run businesses: keep the invoices and receipts in a folder, and once a year — usually in the weeks before the 31 March fiscal year-end — sit down, or hand the folder to an accountant, and turn it all into books.

It feels like the efficient choice. One focused effort instead of a nagging weekly chore. In practice it's one of the more expensive ways to run a small business, and the costs are specific enough to add up.

## What the delay actually costs

**1. You make decisions blind for eleven months.** If the books are a year-end exercise, then for most of the year you don't know your real margin, which customers are slow payers, or whether last quarter was actually profitable. You price jobs, extend credit, and time purchases on feel. Some of those calls are wrong in ways you'd have caught with current numbers, and you never find out which.

**2. Reconstruction costs more than maintenance.** An accountant charging by the hour to rebuild twelve months from a shoebox is slower and dearer than the same accountant reviewing books that were kept as you went. Missing context — "what was this LKR 40,000 transfer?" — has to be chased down months later, often by you, from memory.

**3. VAT and PAYE penalties have already accrued.** VAT returns are filed every period, not annually. If the year-end session is when you discover a return was understated, the [interest and penalties](/blog/vat-return-schedules-explained) have been running since the original deadline. The IRD doesn't wait for your bookkeeping schedule.

**4. Accruals get missed, so the year-end picture is wrong anyway.** The whole point of year-end accounts is a true position — but reconstructing from a folder tends to capture only what was invoiced and paid. Costs incurred but not yet billed, income earned but not yet invoiced, prepayments — these need to have been recorded near the event. Bolted on in March from memory, they're estimates at best.

**5. A missed deadline is a compliance event.** Late statutory accounts and late tax filings are findings in their own right — they affect your standing with the IRD, and they're visible to a lender doing [due diligence](/blog/why-investors-distrust-excel-bookkeeping). "The accounts were three months late every year" is not a neutral fact to a bank.

**6. The scramble has a real opportunity cost.** The February–March period disappears into catch-up bookkeeping — the owner's time, the accountant's premium rates for rushed work, the stress. That's time not spent selling or running the business, at the busiest possible moment to lose it.

## Why "little and often" wins

Books kept close to real time are cheaper to maintain, more accurate, and useful the whole year instead of once. The tasks are small when they're current — record the bill when it arrives, match the bank when the statement comes in, issue the credit note the day you spot the error — and they don't compound. Year-end stops being a reconstruction and becomes a review: check the numbers that are already there, post a handful of adjusting entries, done.
