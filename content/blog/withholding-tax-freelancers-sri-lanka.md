If you've ever paid a freelance designer, a photographer for a shoot, or a consultant for a project, you've probably just paid the invoice in full and moved on. There's a new rule that means, past a certain point, that's no longer correct — and the list of people it applies to is longer than you'd guess.

## What changed

Under the same Inland Revenue (Amendment) Act No. 11 of 2026 that brought in the cash payment rule, effective from 3 June 2026, businesses paying a wide range of professionals more than Rs. 100,000 in a month now have to withhold tax on that payment rather than pay it in full. The list of professions named is broad and, honestly, a little unusual to see grouped together: IT specialists, social media specialists, translators, writers, photographers, videographers, coaches, personal trainers, artists, musicians, dentists, veterinarians, beauticians, event organisers, among others.

If you run a small business, there's a decent chance you already pay someone on that list. A social media manager on retainer. A freelance developer building your site. An events person for your launch. None of these are exotic arrangements — they're the kind of thing almost every SME does at some point, and that's exactly why this rule catches people who assumed withholding tax was something only payroll or big corporate contracts had to think about.

## What "withholding" actually means for you

You don't pay the freelancer the full invoice amount. You deduct a percentage, pay them the rest, and remit the deducted amount to the IRD on their behalf. The freelancer then gets a certificate from you showing what was withheld, which they use to claim it as a credit against their own income tax later.

So a single freelancer payment now has three things attached to it that didn't matter before: the amount withheld, the fact that it has to be remitted, and a certificate that has to reach the freelancer. Miss any one of those and you're the one who's non-compliant, not them.

_(The applicable rate for this specific provision wasn't confirmed in the sources checked for this post — Ledgerly already ships a WHT — Services tax code at 5%, which is the standard rate historically applied to service payments, but confirm the current rate for your situation against ird.gov.lk before relying on it.)_

## Where this actually goes wrong

The threshold is monthly, and that's the part that trips people up. Nobody forgets to withhold on a single Rs. 300,000 invoice — that's obviously over the line. What slips through is the freelancer you pay three or four times a month in smaller amounts: Rs. 40,000 here, Rs. 35,000 there, Rs. 30,000 again two weeks later. Individually none of those looks like it needs anything special. Add them up over the month and you've crossed Rs. 100,000 without anyone noticing, because nothing forced you to look at the total.

That's a bookkeeping failure more than a tax one. If your freelancer payments live in a WhatsApp thread and a bank statement, there's no natural point where the running total surfaces itself. You only find out when it's already been missed.

## Where Ledgerly fits

Ledgerly already ships with a **WHT — Services** tax code, seeded alongside VAT and SSCL, because withholding on service payments isn't new — this amendment just widened who it applies to. Put that code on a bill line from a contractor and the withheld amount posts to its own account instead of quietly disappearing into the expense total, so what you owe the supplier and what you owe the IRD stay visible as two separate numbers rather than one net figure nobody can unpick later.

More importantly, because every bill is tied to a specific [supplier](/guide/purchases/suppliers), you can actually see what you've paid a given freelancer this month, added up, rather than reconstructing it from memory or a folder of invoices. If the rate for your situation differs from the seeded 5%, tax codes carry [dated rates](/guide/accounting/tax-codes) you can update without touching past bills. It doesn't decide who's in scope for you — that's still a judgement call worth checking with an advisor — but it stops the threshold from being invisible.
