If you're starting a business in Sri Lanka, you'll read plenty about choosing between a **sole proprietorship** and a **private limited company** — limited liability, tax rates, how investors see each one. What those guides skip is the part you live with every month afterwards: the two structures impose genuinely different bookkeeping obligations. Here's what actually changes.

## Sole proprietorship: lighter, but not "no books"

A sole proprietorship isn't a separate legal person. You and the business are the same entity; the profit is your income.

**What that means for the books:**

- **No statutory audit, no filed financial statements.** You aren't required to lodge accounts with the Registrar of Companies.
- **Records still required for tax.** You file personal income tax on the business profit, so you need enough of a record — income, expenses, assets — to support that return, and to keep it for the statutory retention period. The IRD can still ask.
- **VAT is the real trigger.** The moment your taxable turnover crosses the [VAT registration threshold](/blog/vat-registration-sri-lanka-guide) — or you register voluntarily — your obligations jump to the same level as any company: [gazette-format tax invoices](/blog/gazette-2481-22-explained), periodic [VAT returns](/blog/vat-return-schedules-explained), input-tax records. VAT doesn't care what structure you are.
- **The trap: mixed finances.** Because there's no legal separation, sole proprietors routinely run business and personal money through one account. It's not illegal, but it makes the books a reconstruction exercise and any future review painful.

## Private limited company: a separate entity, with the obligations that follow

A private limited company under the **Companies Act No. 7 of 2007** is its own legal person. Its money is not your money.

**What that means for the books:**

- **Proper accounting records are a legal duty.** The Act requires records that correctly explain the company's transactions and let financial statements be prepared — for **every** company, from day one.
- **Annual financial statements, and usually an audit.** The company prepares statements under the applicable standard (SLFRS / SLFRS for SMEs) each year, and unless it qualifies for the small-company audit exemption, they must be [audited](/blog/sme-audit-requirements-sri-lanka).
- **Statutory filings.** Annual return to the Registrar, maintained registers, board and shareholder records.
- **A hard line between company and personal.** Owner money going in is capital or a director's loan; money coming out is salary, dividends, or loan repayment — each with its own tax treatment and its own ledger account. "I took LKR 100,000 out" has to be classified, not just recorded.
- **Equity actually matters.** Share capital, retained earnings, dividends, and the [statement of changes in equity](/guide/reports/changes-in-equity) — a section a sole proprietor can largely ignore — become real, reportable parts of the accounts.

## Side by side

|                                 | Sole proprietor  | Private limited                               |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Separate legal entity           | No               | Yes                                           |
| Statutory audit                 | No               | Yes, unless exempt                            |
| Filed annual accounts           | No               | Yes                                           |
| Records for tax                 | Yes              | Yes                                           |
| VAT obligations (if registered) | Full             | Full                                          |
| Owner draws                     | Just withdrawals | Salary / dividend / loan — must be classified |
| Equity accounting               | Minimal          | Full                                          |

## The practical implication

If you expect to incorporate later — most growing businesses do — the useful move is to keep books that are **already structured to company standard** while you're still a sole proprietor: business-only bank account, double-entry, issued documents that lock, periods that close. Then incorporation is a matter of opening the company's books and carrying balances over, not rebuilding your bookkeeping habits under time pressure.

Ledgerly runs the same way for both structures — double-entry, immutable posted documents, a real equity section when you need one — so the ledger you keep as a sole proprietor is the ledger a company would keep.
