Say you buy a batch of stock from a supplier for Rs. 600,000 and pay cash on delivery, because that's how you've always done it with that supplier. You get a receipt, you record the purchase, life goes on. Under a change that came into force earlier this year, that receipt might not be worth what you think it's worth at tax time.

## What actually changed

The Inland Revenue (Amendment) Act No. 11 of 2026, certified on 3 June 2026, added a rule that's easy to miss because it doesn't touch VAT or invoice formats — it touches deductibility. If you pay Rs. 500,000 or more to someone in cash, or by any method other than a short list of approved ones, that payment can't be claimed as a deductible business expense. If it was meant to be the cost of an asset, it can't go into that asset's tax cost either. The expense still happened, the goods or services still exist, but for tax purposes it's as if the deduction never occurred.

The approved methods are the ones you'd expect: an account payee cheque, a bank draft, a credit or debit card, an electronic bank transfer, or cash deposited directly into the recipient's own bank account. That last one is worth noting on its own, because it means the rule isn't really about cash as physical notes — it's about whether the payment leaves a bank-verifiable trail. Handing someone Rs. 500,000 in an envelope fails the test. Walking into a bank and depositing Rs. 500,000 straight into their account passes it, even though it's still cash.

The threshold applies per transaction, but also to a single day's payments and to a series of payments tied to the same event — so paying Rs. 300,000 on Monday and Rs. 300,000 on Wednesday for the same order doesn't get you around it.

## Why this is a bookkeeping problem more than a tax problem

Most advice on this says "pay by bank transfer." Fine, but the harder question for a business that's been running on cash is: **how do you even know which of your past payments would fail the test?** That's not a tax question, it's a records question — you need to be able to look at any payment over the threshold and say, immediately, how it was made.

A lot of small business bookkeeping doesn't actually capture that. A bill gets marked "paid" and the method disappears into the transaction — nobody goes back and separately notes whether it was cash, transfer, or card. If your only record is "paid: yes," you've lost the one fact that now decides whether the deduction survives.

This is the same underlying issue behind [why banks and investors don't trust Excel](/blog/why-investors-distrust-excel-bookkeeping): a spreadsheet can say whatever the last person typed, and if payment method was never a required field, it just isn't there for the payments that mattered.

## What good records look like here

The habit that protects you isn't complicated. Every payment you make should record, at minimum: how much, to whom, when, and through which account or method it left. That last part is the one people skip, and it's the one this rule is built around.

For payments that land near or over the Rs. 500,000 line, it's worth being deliberate rather than reactive — use a transfer by default for larger supplier payments, and if cash genuinely has to change hands, deposit it into the supplier's account rather than handing it over directly. Cheaper than losing the deduction later.

## Where Ledgerly fits

Every [supplier payment](/guide/purchases/supplier-payments) in Ledgerly records a **method** and which **bank or cash account** the money actually left from — Cash in Hand is a distinct account from your bank accounts, not a checkbox that gets forgotten. The same is true for a [direct expense](/guide/purchases/direct-expenses) paid on the spot: it names the account the spend came out of. Because that's captured on every transaction as a matter of course, not as a special field someone has to remember to fill in, a Rs. 500,000-plus payment made in cash is visible in your books as exactly that — not hidden inside a generic "paid" status you'd have to go digging through supplier folders to reconstruct.

It won't stop you paying in cash if that's what the situation calls for. It just means you — or your accountant, at return time — can see it coming rather than finding out during an assessment.
