Plenty of articles walk you through the mechanics of registering for VAT with the Inland Revenue Department. This one covers those briefly — then spends most of its time on the question they skip: **once you're registered, what are you actually on the hook to do?** That's where new businesses get caught, not on the registration form itself.

## When you have to register

You must register for VAT when your taxable supplies cross the registration threshold — measured over a rolling period the VAT Act defines. The threshold and the measurement window get revised in budget cycles, so check the **current** figure on **ird.gov.lk** rather than relying on a number you saw somewhere; getting this wrong in either direction is costly.

You can also register **voluntarily** below the threshold, which can make sense if your customers are themselves VAT-registered businesses that reclaim input tax, or if you're incurring significant input VAT on setup costs you'd like to recover. And there's **temporary VAT registration**, used for one-off or project-based activity (common with construction contracts and certain imports) rather than an ongoing business.

## What you'll need to register

The usual set:

- **Business registration** documents — the certificate of incorporation for a company, or the business-name registration for a sole proprietorship or partnership.
- **Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN)** for the business.
- Bank account details, a description of business activity, and expected turnover.
- For a company: details of directors; for other structures, the proprietor or partners.
- Proof of business address.

Registration is done through the IRD, increasingly via the RAMIS portal, and you're issued a **VAT registration number** once approved.

## What registration actually obligates you to produce

This is the part worth reading twice. From the effective date of your registration, you are required to:

**1. Issue tax invoices in the gazette format.** Every sale to another business now needs a compliant **tax invoice** — not a receipt, not an informal bill. From 1 July 2026, [Gazette 2481/22](/blog/gazette-2481-22-explained) sets the mandatory format: the heading "TAX INVOICE", your VAT number and the customer's TIN, a **supply date** as a field separate from the invoice date, tax broken out per line and in total, and a gapless invoice number. A generic invoicing app that predates the gazette often can't represent the supply date correctly, so this is worth checking before you commit to a tool.

**2. Charge and account for output tax.** You add VAT at the applicable rate to your taxable sales, and that money is the government's — it sits in your books as a liability until you remit it, not as revenue.

**3. Track input tax separately.** VAT you pay on business purchases is generally recoverable, but only if you hold a valid tax invoice for it and it's correctly recorded. Sloppy purchase records directly cost you refunds.

**4. File a VAT return every period.** On a recurring schedule, you file a return that reports your output tax, your input tax, and the net payable or refundable — broken out across the IRD's [return schedules](/blog/vat-return-schedules-explained) for standard-rated, zero-rated, and exempt supplies, plus credit and debit notes. Miss the deadline and penalties accrue automatically.

**5. Keep it all for seven years.** VAT invoices and supporting records must be retained for the statutory period and produced on request.

## The practical takeaway

Registering for VAT turns your bookkeeping from optional to obligatory. The businesses that handle it well are the ones that set up structured books **at registration**, not the ones that keep using a spreadsheet and try to reconstruct everything at return time — which is its own [expensive mistake](/blog/year-end-bookkeeping-catch-up-cost).
