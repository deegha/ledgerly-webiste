If you sell, distribute, or import electrical goods in Sri Lanka, last week's news should get your attention even if nobody's knocked on your door yet.

## What happened

The Consumer Affairs Authority ran an islandwide raid programme across **25–29 August and 8–9 September 2026** — 204 raids, over **1,150 items seized**, on top of more than 1,000 raids the CAA has already carried out on electrical and electronic goods this year. The products swept up read like a typical hardware-shop inventory: extension cords, multi-sockets, plug tops, switch sockets, wires, LED bulbs, chargers, adapters, torches, scales, kettles, irons, electrical tools.

The violations weren't mainly about dangerous products slipping through. They were about **missing paperwork**:

- No bills, receipts, or invoices a trader could produce on request.
- No way to show where the stock actually came from.
- Missing or incorrect product labelling.
- No warranty, or no written warranty certificate, for goods that require a **minimum six-month warranty**.

The CAA has said it intends to trace supply chains under **Direction No. 83** and pursue retailers, suppliers, distributors, importers, and manufacturers up and down that chain — not just the shop that got raided. _(Newswire's report didn't specify penalty amounts or a compliance deadline — this is an active enforcement campaign, not a one-off notice, so check ncpc.gov.lk / the CAA's own notices before assuming anything is settled.)_

## Why this is a bookkeeping story, not just a compliance one

Read the list of violations again: no bills, no proof of source, no warranty record. Every one of those is a question your **books** are supposed to be able to answer instantly. A raid is, functionally, an audit with worse timing — and the traders who got caught out are the ones whose records couldn't answer basic questions on the spot.

Three things the CAA is asking for map directly onto ordinary bookkeeping discipline:

**"Where did this stock come from?"** is answered by a supplier bill. If every batch of chargers or extension cords you hold was recorded against a [supplier](/guide/purchases/suppliers) — with their name, TIN, the date, the quantity — you have your supply chain on record before anyone asks. If stock just "arrived" with no bill entered because it was paid for in cash and nobody typed it in, you have exactly the gap the CAA is finding.

**"Prove you sold this item, on this date, with this warranty."** is answered by the invoice or receipt you issued. A proper sale record — item, customer, date, price — is also what starts the six-month warranty clock and is the thing a customer needs to invoke it later. [Recording sales through informal notes or verbal promises](/blog/bookkeeping-mistakes-sri-lankan-smes) instead of issued documents is the same underlying failure as not recording a bill: a transaction happened, and nothing durable says so.

**"Prove these records weren't put together after the fact."** is the one most traders haven't thought about. If your "proof" is a document you can still edit, a CAA officer — or, later, an investor or an auditor — has no way to know whether it was created honestly at the time of sale or assembled afterward to look tidy. This is the same [immutability](/blog/what-immutable-means-in-accounting-software) question that comes up with [banks and investors](/blog/why-investors-distrust-excel-bookkeeping): a record that _can_ be backdated is worth less than one that structurally _can't_ be.

## What this looks like done properly

None of this requires new processes invented for the CAA specifically — it's what [double-entry bookkeeping](/blog/double-entry-bookkeeping-for-non-accountants) already asks of you, applied to a sector that's currently being checked hard:

- Every consignment of stock is entered as a [supplier bill](/guide/purchases/recording-a-bill) the day it arrives — supplier, TIN, quantity, item — not reconstructed later from memory.
- Every sale is an issued invoice or receipt with a real date, tied to the item sold, so the six-month warranty period is unambiguous and on record.
- Both are timestamped and locked once posted, so "we've always kept proper records" is something you can actually demonstrate, not just claim.

## Where Ledgerly fits

Ledgerly doesn't print warranty cards or product labels — those are still your job. What it gives you is the underlying evidence trail the CAA is checking for, produced as a by-product of normal bookkeeping rather than a scramble before an inspection:

- Every bill is tied to a named [supplier](/guide/purchases/suppliers) with their TIN, dated the day the stock arrived — your supply-chain proof, already on file.
- Every [invoice](/guide/sales/creating-an-invoice) records the item, the customer, and the sale date, so warranty start dates aren't in dispute.
- Posted bills and invoices are [immutable](/blog/what-immutable-means-in-accounting-software) — once entered, they can't be quietly edited, so the paper trail you hand over is provably the one created at the time, not one assembled after the fact.

If an officer — or a bank, or an auditor — asks "can you prove where this came from and when you sold it," the honest answer should take thirty seconds to produce, not a weekend of digging through a shoebox.
