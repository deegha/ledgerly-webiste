A lot of small manufacturers in Sri Lanka run their books on an invoicing app plus a spreadsheet. It works, right up until someone asks a question the setup can't answer: _what did it actually cost us to make this?_

## The problem with treating a maker like a reseller

An invoicing app models a simple world: you buy an item, you sell the same item, the difference is your margin. A manufacturer breaks that model. You buy cloth and buttons; you sell shirts. The shirt was never purchased, so it has no cost history. The cloth was purchased, but it doesn't leave your books when it becomes a shirt — it just sits there.

So three things quietly go wrong:

- **Your inventory is misstated.** Raw materials you've already consumed still show as stock. Finished goods you're holding show as nothing.
- **Your gross margin is a guess.** Cost of sales on a shirt is whatever someone estimated, not a figure built from the materials that went into it.
- **Your auditor can't trace it.** "How did you arrive at the value of finished goods?" has no mechanical answer — it's a workpaper someone maintains by hand.

## What a ledger-based approach does instead

The fix isn't a bigger spreadsheet. It's treating the moment materials become product as a real transaction — a **production order** — that:

1. relieves the raw materials from stock at their actual cost;
2. adds the finished goods to stock at the sum of those costs;
3. posts a journal entry recording exactly that.

Now the shirt has a cost, derived from real purchase prices, frozen at the moment it was made. Sell it and cost of sales is that number. Your balance sheet shows raw materials, and finished goods, as separate real values. And "how did you value finished goods" has the same answer every other number in a double-entry system has: it's the sum of these posted entries, each attached to the document that created it.

## Why this matters more in Sri Lanka right now

Compliance expectations are rising — the [gazette invoice format](/blog/gazette-2481-22-explained), RAMIS transmission, tighter audit scrutiny under SLAuS. A manufacturer whose finished-goods valuation is a manual estimate is carrying a finding waiting to happen. One whose valuation is computed from the ledger isn't.

You don't need a factory ERP. You need your bookkeeping to understand that you make things — so the cost of making them is recorded, not reconstructed.
