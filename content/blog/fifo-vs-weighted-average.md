If you carry inventory, every sale needs a cost attached to it — not just a selling price. That cost is what turns a sale into a P&L entry with a real gross margin, and how you calculate it is a real accounting decision, not a technical detail.

## The two methods, briefly

**Weighted average** blends the cost of everything you're currently holding into a single per-unit cost, and recalculates that blend every time new stock comes in. Simple, stable, and it smooths out price fluctuations — which is usually exactly what you want for goods where individual units are interchangeable (raw materials, common hardware, anything bought in bulk at slightly different prices over time).

**FIFO** (first-in, first-out) assumes you sell your oldest stock first, and costs each sale using the price you actually paid for those specific units. It's the better fit when unit cost genuinely tracks _when_ something was bought — perishables, anything with real price volatility, or stock where you want the cost of goods sold to reflect actual purchase history rather than a blend.

## Neither is "more correct" — but the mismatch is a real error

The mistake isn't picking the "wrong" one — both are standard, auditable methods. The mistake is using **one method for your whole business** when your inventory doesn't behave uniformly. A hardware distributor might have a slow-moving, price-stable line where weighted average is fine, and a fast-moving line with real cost swings where FIFO tells a truer story. Forcing both through the same method either overstates or understates margin on one side or the other — quietly, every month, in a way that's hard to catch after the fact.

## What to look for in your books

- **Can you set costing method per item**, not just once for the whole company? If not, you're compromising on at least some of your inventory.
- **Does a sale automatically post the matching COGS entry** the moment it happens, using whichever method that item is set to? If COGS is a manual journal entry someone remembers to make at month-end, it's not really tied to the sale — it's a guess reconciled later.
- **What happens when you sell stock the system doesn't show you holding?** The safe default is to block the sale, not silently let stock go negative — a negative stock balance is usually a sign something upstream (a missed goods receipt, a wrong item code) is wrong, not a real inventory state.

Getting this right doesn't just make your gross margin more accurate — it's the difference between a stock valuation your auditor can trace to source and one they have to take on faith.
