If you only ever open two reports outside of tax season, make them the **AR ageing** and the **AP ageing**. Between them they tell you who owes you money and how late they are, and who you owe and when it's due — which is most of what running out of cash comes down to. Here's how to read them.

## What "ageing" means

Both reports take a list of unpaid amounts and sort them by **how long they've been outstanding**, grouped into time bands — the _ageing buckets_. A typical layout:

|                     | Current     | 1–30 days             | 31–60 days | 61–90 days | 90+ days          |
| ------------------- | ----------- | --------------------- | ---------- | ---------- | ----------------- |
| Customer / Supplier | not yet due | overdue up to a month | …          | …          | seriously overdue |

Each row is a customer (AR) or supplier (AP); each column is a bucket; the number in a cell is how much of that party's balance falls in that band. The far-right column is the one to watch.

## AR ageing — who owes you, and how worried to be

**AR** is accounts _receivable_ — money customers owe you against invoices you've issued.

The report answers, at a glance:

- **How much is genuinely overdue** vs merely outstanding. The "Current" column isn't a problem — those invoices aren't due yet. Everything to the right of it is.
- **Which customers are your collection risk.** A big balance sitting in 90+ is a customer who may not pay at all. That's a conversation, possibly a credit hold, possibly a provision for doubtful debt.
- **Whether your terms are working.** If most of your balance is in 31–60 when your terms are 30 days, customers are treating your invoices as 60-day credit and you're financing them.
- **Your realistic near-term cash-in.** Current plus 1–30, discounted a bit, is roughly what's actually coming.

A practical habit: each week, work the report right-to-left — chase the 90+ first, then 61–90. The [overdue reminders](/guide/sales/overdue-reminders) feature can automate the early nudges so you only spend time on the hard cases.

## AP ageing — who you owe, and when

**AP** is accounts _payable_ — money you owe suppliers against [bills you've recorded](/blog/invoice-quote-bill-expense-explained).

It answers:

- **What's due this week, this month.** Your upcoming cash-out, laid out by timing, so a payment run doesn't surprise you.
- **Whether you're quietly late.** Balances drifting into 31–60 and beyond mean you're straining supplier relationships — the ones you'll need when you want credit or a rush order.
- **Where you have slack.** If cash is tight, the report shows which payables are still in "Current" and can wait a few days without breaking anything.

The AP ageing only works if bills are entered when they _arrive_, not when you pay them. If you record bills on payment day, this report is always empty and always useless.

## Reading them together

The two side by side are a short-term cash forecast. If your AR ageing shows LKR 800,000 coming in over the next 30 days and your AP ageing shows LKR 1,100,000 going out in the same window, you have a gap to close now — collect faster, stretch a payable, or arrange finance — while you still have options. Leave both reports unread and you find out on the day.

This is also where the [ageing reports feed an audit](/blog/sme-audit-requirements-sri-lanka): the totals must agree to the receivables and payables control accounts on your balance sheet, and an auditor will check that they do.
